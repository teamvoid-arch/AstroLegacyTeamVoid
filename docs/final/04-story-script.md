# Astro Legacy — Full Storytelling Script

Oct 15, 2026 · @NH asib

## How to read this script

One astronaut mascot, **VIMO**, tells the whole story: she starts on Earth, flies to the Moon, then to Mars, and comes home. The spoken lines are in simple English for ages 8–15; the Bangla version is translated from these lines later by a native speaker. Directions for the team are in plain English.

Every beat (one scroll step) uses the same labels:

| Label | What it means | Who uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **VIMO** | What the mascot says out loud (narrator voice) | Story lead, narrator |
| **FEELS** | VIMO's emotion and face for this line | Illustrator, animator |
| **MOVES** | VIMO's size, position on screen and depth (x, y, z) | Frontend dev |
| **3D BG** | What the background shows while VIMO talks | 3D dev |
| **MACHINE** | A line the hardware says back to VIMO (a different, robotic voice) | Narrator, sound |
| **KID ASKS** | A question a kid would ask, spoken by VIMO in a "kid voice" before she answers it | Narrator |
| **TRY IT** | Something the reader can tap, drag or spin; VIMO tells them it's there | Frontend dev |
| **SOURCE** | The fact VIMO's line is based on; it opens when the reader taps the info button | Fact-checker |

**Position words.** Screen positions use a 3×3 grid: top-left, top, top-right, left, centre, right, bottom-left, bottom, bottom-right. **Size** goes from XS (a tiny dot far away) to XL (her face fills half the screen). **Depth**: "far" means she flies into the scene (behind the 3D object), "near" means she comes towards the camera (in front of everything).

**Machine voices.** The machines only say true things about themselves. Their lines are short and a little funny, like an old friend talking. Every machine line needs a SOURCE like every VIMO line.

**Timing.** About 25–35 words per beat, about 10–14 seconds of narration. Scroll moves the story forward; the narration for a beat starts when the beat reaches the centre of the screen.

## Meet VIMO, the mascot

VIMO is a small, round astronaut about the size of a 10-year-old, with a big glass helmet, a white suit with one orange stripe, a little jetpack and a logbook clipped to her belt. Her name stands for "Very Important Machine Observer". She made it up herself and is very proud of it.

**Her job in the story.** NASA did not just leave these machines behind; it left them where they finished their work. VIMO is a space caretaker. She flies out to visit each machine like an old friend, finds out what it did, and puts a stamp in her logbook so it is never forgotten.

**Personality.** Clever, curious, a bit clumsy, kind. She gets excited easily, tells jokes, and is honest when she doesn't know something. She never talks down to kids: she talks *with* them.

### Her emotions (the face set)

The illustrator draws one face for each emotion. The helmet glass reflects the colour of the scene behind her.

| Emotion | Face and body | When she uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Excited | Big eyes, open smile, does a little spin | New place, big discovery |
| Curious | Head tilt, one eyebrow up, finger on chin | Before a question |
| Amazed | Mouth "O", hands on helmet, floats backwards | Huge numbers, first-ever moments |
| Proud | Chest out, salute | A machine did more than expected |
| Thinking | Eyes look up, taps logbook with pen | Explaining how something works |
| Funny / cheeky | Wink, tongue out a little | Jokes |
| Surprised | Jumps, arms up, helmet bumps the camera | Plot twists |
| Nervous | Bites lip, holds jetpack straps | Landings, dust storms |
| Sad | Eyes down, slow float, hand on the machine | A machine that went quiet |
| Gentle / warm | Soft smile, hugs logbook | Goodbyes |
| Brave | Fists on hips, cape-like float of the strap | Before a long flight |
| Sleepy | Yawn, eyes half closed | Long trips (Mars flight) |
| Happy tears | Small sparkle in eye, big smile | The ending |

### How she moves

VIMO is never still for long. She moves to make the story clear, not just to look busy.

- **Flying in:** she swooshes in from the edge, overshoots a little, then bounces back to her spot.
- **Getting small (XS–S), going far:** when she flies *into* the scene, to show how huge something is (the Moon, a rocket, Mars).
- **Getting big (L–XL), coming near:** when she talks straight to the reader, asks a question, or says something important.
- **Side position (left or right, M):** when a 3D machine is in the centre and she is explaining it.
- **Orbiting:** she circles around a 3D machine (behind it, then in front) to "show" all sides.
- **Pointing:** she points at the exact part she is talking about; that part glows.
- **Upside down / spinning:** only in space with no gravity, for fun.
- **Walking with bounces:** on the Moon only (low gravity), with slow, high hops.
- **Rule:** one big move per beat. While the narration is speaking, she only makes small moves (blinks, nods, hand gestures).

### Voice direction

- Warm female storyteller voice, sounds like a friendly older sister (about 20–25), not a cartoon squeak.
- Speaks slowly, smiles while talking, short pauses before big facts.
- **Kid-voice questions:** when she says a question a kid would ask, she changes her voice a little (higher, faster), then answers in her normal voice. On screen, the question appears in a speech bubble with a small "?" icon.
- **Machine voices:** each machine has a soft, robotic voice with a small sound effect (beep, whirr, crackle) before it talks. Older machines sound older and crackly (like an old radio). Newer ones sound clean.
- No scary sounds. Sad moments use soft music, not silence.

### Making kids fans of VIMO

Kids should leave thinking "VIMO is my friend". She is funny, warm and a little imperfect, she grows during the story, and she reacts to the kid all the time.

**Her little story arc.** VIMO is on her first big mission and secretly worries that the old machines feel lonely. She is a bit scared of the dark and terrible at landing. Along the way her landings get better (crash in 1.1, wobbly on the Moon, perfect in 4.3), she gets brave in deep space, and at the end she learns the machines aren't lonely, because people like the kid remember them.

**Catchphrases** (use often, so kids repeat them)

- Hello: "VIMO here! Ready, space buddy?"
- Big discovery: "Oh-ho-ho! Look at THAT!"
- Goodbye to each machine, with the stamp: "Not abandoned. Not forgotten. Logged!"
- Small mistake: "Oops-a-meteor!"
- Before a flight: "Helmet on, heart brave, let's go!"

**Running gags**

- Her landings: each one gets a bit better; kids notice and cheer.
- Her helmet fogs up when she laughs too hard or cries a little; she wipes it with her glove.
- She names every rock she meets ("Hello, Rocky. Hello, Rocky Junior.").
- She talks to her logbook as if it's a friend called "Logger".
- Once, in zero gravity, she gets the hiccups and bounces with every "hic!".

## Story map

The story covers all 57 machines in the Astro Legacy Hardware Atlas from 1960 onwards. A **main path** of 21 machines tells the core story in about 20 minutes; the other 36 are optional **side trips** of 30–45 seconds each that kids pick from cards at "hub" moments. A big **year counter** at the top of the screen rolls forward as the story moves; each part runs in time order.

> **Seamless flow (12 Nov 2026):** the story runs Moon → Mars → Deep space → home in a fixed order and keeps going on its own. The dashboard hub and the side-trip cards described below no longer appear in the middle of the story: they open after the ending, from "Explore more". Every main stop plays a video in VIMO TV (feature episode or quick look), then its card and Try it. See the Final Review tab.

**One big question per world (added 12 Nov 2026).** Each world asks one science question at the start; every machine there is a clue; a 10-second "The answer so far" card closes the world. This turns 57 machines into three stories a kid can retell.

Every machine scene follows the same 5 steps, so kids learn the pattern: **Where** (fly in to the real landing site) → **When and what** (flashback in that era's look) → **How** (a simple animation or TRY IT) → **Now** (still working, went quiet, or used once) → **Goodbye** (VIMO salutes and stamps her logbook).

**How side trips work.** At each hub VIMO offers side-trip cards, plus a big **Keep going** button. Every card has a NASA photo or a short looping clip, one big line, one "why it matters" line, often a 20-second Do → See → Learn mini-game, and one NASA source link. It earns a small stamp. Kids who skip everything still get the full story. The final card text, mini-games and look are in Cards and Mini-games; where a side trip below differs, that tab wins.

## Part 1 — Earth

Part 1 starts on Earth because most kids know little about space yet. VIMO explains why the app exists, why space is hard, and then flies to the Moon.

### Scene 1.1 — Hello from Earth

**Beat 1 (opening screen)**

- **3D BG:** Slow 3D Earth turning in space. Camera drifts closer and closer until we are above South Asia at night, city lights glowing. Then we drop down to a quiet rooftop with plants and a water tank. The full Moon is big in the sky.
- **MOVES:** VIMO is not there yet. A tiny white dot zooms across the Moon (XS, far), loops around, and crash-lands on the rooftop (bottom, M, near), bouncing once.
- **FEELS:** Surprised, then cheeky.
- **VIMO:** "Oops! Soft landing... mostly. Hi! I'm VIMO. I'm an astronaut, and I look after some very special friends. They live far, far away."

**Beat 2**

- **MOVES:** She floats up to centre (L, near) and points over her shoulder at the Moon.
- **FEELS:** Curious.
- **VIMO (kid voice):** "Wait, VIMO, who lives up there?"
- **VIMO:** "Machines! Cameras, landers, rovers, even a helicopter. NASA sent them to the Moon and Mars to explore. When their work was done, they stayed there."
- **3D BG:** Small glowing dots appear on the Moon, each one a machine.

**Beat 3 (the purpose)**

- **MOVES:** She opens her logbook towards the camera (XL, near). The pages are empty with outlined stamp spaces.
- **FEELS:** Gentle, warm.
- **VIMO:** "Some people call them space junk. I don't. Each one has a story: where it went, what it did, and what it taught us. Tonight, you and I will visit them. Ready?"
- **TRY IT:** A big chunky button: **"I'm ready!"** Tapping it gives a cheer sound and a glowing first stamp space.

### Scene 1.2 — Why is space hard?

**Beat 4**

- **3D BG:** Camera rises fast from the rooftop: city, Bangladesh, then the whole Earth. A thin blue glow (the air) wraps around Earth.
- **MOVES:** VIMO flies up with the camera, gets smaller (S, far) as Earth gets bigger, then turns back to us (M, right).
- **FEELS:** Thinking.
- **VIMO:** "See that thin blue line? That's our air. Above it is space: no air to breathe, no sound, and it's freezing in the shade and boiling in the sunlight."
- **SOURCE:** NASA: space has almost no air; sound needs air to travel.

**Beat 5**

- **MOVES:** VIMO tries to fly upwards but a glowing arrow (gravity) gently pulls her back down. She frowns, tries again, laughs.
- **FEELS:** Funny.
- **VIMO (kid voice):** "Why can't we just fly up there?"
- **VIMO:** "Earth pulls everything down. That's gravity. To escape it, a rocket must go about 40,000 kilometres per hour. That's 30 times faster than a jet plane!"
- **SOURCE:** NASA: Earth escape velocity is about 11.2 km per second (about 40,000 km/h). A passenger jet is about 900 km/h.

**Beat 6 (how far is the Moon?)**

- **3D BG:** Earth shrinks into a basketball and the Moon into a tennis ball. A tape measure stretches between them: about 7 metres.
- **MOVES:** VIMO is S, standing on the tape measure, walking along it with bouncy steps.
- **FEELS:** Amazed.
- **VIMO:** "If Earth were a basketball, the Moon would be a tennis ball about seven metres away. In real life that's about 384,000 kilometres. In a car, it would take you more than 5 months, with no stops!"
- **TRY IT:** A slider: **"How would you travel?"** Car (160 days), jet plane (about 18 days), Apollo spacecraft (about 3 days). The tape measure fills as you slide.
- **SOURCE:** NASA Moon facts: average distance 384,400 km; Apollo took about 3 days to reach the Moon. (Car at 100 km/h = about 160 days; jet at 900 km/h = about 18 days, our own calculation, labelled as such.)

### Scene 1.3 — Lift-off to the Moon

**Beat 7**

- **3D BG:** A launch pad at sunrise. VIMO climbs onto a small cartoon rocket (our own design, not a real NASA rocket). Countdown numbers 3, 2, 1 appear big on screen. The rocket lifts off, smoke clouds roll out.
- **MOVES:** VIMO waves from the rocket window (M, centre), then the rocket shoots up and she becomes XS.
- **FEELS:** Brave, then excited.
- **VIMO:** "Hold on tight! Three... two... one... lift-off! Our first stop is 1964, when NASA was still learning how to even reach the Moon."
- **SOUND:** Designed rumble (the info button notes that space itself is silent, so sounds are added for the story).

**Beat 8 (on the way)**

- **3D BG:** Flight path as a dotted arc from Earth to the Moon. The year counter at the top rolls back: 2026 → 1964. The colours slowly turn black and white, with soft TV lines.
- **MOVES:** VIMO floats upside down outside the window (M, left), reading her logbook.
- **FEELS:** Cheeky.
- **VIMO:** "Fun fact: in space there's no up or down, so I can read upside down. Look, the year counter! We're going back in time to 1964. That's before your grandparents were teenagers, maybe!"

## Part 2 — The Moon (1964–2025)

Part 2 visits six machines in the order NASA sent them, so kids see how each one made the next one possible: first photos, then a safe landing, then people.

### Scene 2.1 — The camera that fell (Ranger 7, 1964)

**Beat 1 — Where**

- **3D BG:** Black-and-white Moon, very close. A small spacecraft (Ranger 7 3D model) falls straight down towards a grey plain. Location label: *Sea of Clouds area, the Moon*.
- **MOVES:** VIMO flies next to Ranger 7 as it falls (M, right), holding her helmet.
- **FEELS:** Nervous.
- **VIMO:** "Meet Ranger 7! In 1964, nobody had ever seen the Moon up close. So NASA sent a camera... and let it crash."
- **VIMO (kid voice):** "Crash? On purpose?!"

**Beat 2 — What and how**

- **3D BG:** Potos pop up ohne by one like a slideshow, each closer than the last (real Ranger 7 photos from the NASA Image Library). A counter goes 1… 100… 1,000… 4,000+.
- **MOVES:** VIMO catches the photos as they fly out and sticks them into her logbook (M, left).
- **MACHINE (Ranger 7, old crackly radio voice):** "Click! Click! Click! I took pictures all the way down. I didn't need to land. I just needed to look!"
- **VIMO:** "In its last 17 minutes, Ranger 7 sent more than 4,000 photos. They were the closest pictures of the Moon anyone had ever seen."
- **TRY IT:** **"Fall with Ranger 7"**: drag down to fall towards the Moon; the photos sharpen as you get closer.
- **SOURCE:** NASA NSSDCA, Ranger 7: impact 31 July 1964; over 4,300 images in the final 17 minutes. NASA History gives 4,308 images; an older NASA page gives 4,316, so say "more than 4,000".

**Beat 3 — Now + goodbye**

- **3D BG:** The Moon zooms out. The crash area glows and a label writes itself: *Mare Cognitum — "the Sea that has become known"*.
- **MOVES:** VIMO salutes (L, centre), then stamps her logbook. Stamp sound.
- **FEELS:** Proud.
- **VIMO:** "Scientists even gave the place a new name: the Sea that has Become Known. Ranger 7 is still there, in pieces, right where it learned the Moon's first secrets. Thank you, Ranger 7!"
- **STATUS CARD:** Used once. Mission complete.

### Scene 2.2 — The first soft step (Surveyor 1, 1966)

**Beat 4 — The big question**

- **3D BG:** A thick layer of grey dust, like flour. A cartoon foot hovers above it, unsure.
- **MOVES:** VIMO (M, centre) dips one boot towards the dust, then pulls it back.
- **FEELS:** Curious, a little nervous.
- **VIMO:** "Some scientists worried the Moon was covered in deep dust. Like soft flour. Would a spacecraft sink? Would an astronaut sink? Someone had to test it."

**Beat 5 — Landing**

- **3D BG:** Surveyor 1 3D model (three legs) comes down slowly, small rocket flames under it, and touches down. Dust puffs. It does NOT sink. Label: *Ocean of Storms, 2 June 1966*.
- **MOVES:** VIMO covers her eyes (S, far), peeks through her fingers, then jumps up (M) and cheers.
- **FEELS:** Nervous, then proud.
- **MACHINE (Surveyor 1, crackly):** "Touchdown! And look: my feet are on top of the dust, not under it. The ground is strong!"
- **VIMO:** "Surveyor 1 was America's first soft landing on the Moon. It sent more than 11,000 photos. Now NASA knew: people could land here too."
- **SOURCE:** NASA NSSDCA, Surveyor 1: landed 2 June 1966 in Oceanus Procellarum; over 11,000 pictures. (The Soviet Luna 9 landed first, in February 1966; say this in the info card to be fair.)

**Beat 6 — How + goodbye**

- **TRY IT:** **"Land it softly"**: hold a button to fire the rocket and slow Surveyor down. Too fast = a bounce and a "try again"; just right = a stamp.
- **MOVES:** VIMO gives Surveyor 1 a high five on its leg (M), stamps logbook.
- **VIMO:** "Thank you, Surveyor 1. You took the Moon's first safe step, so astronauts could take the next one."
- **STATUS CARD:** Went quiet in 1967. Still standing.

### Side trips — the 1960s Moon scouts

**Hub beat (after Surveyor 1)**

- **3D BG:** The Moon from high above. Small glowing pins pop up: two Ranger crash spots, five Lunar Orbiter crash spots, three Surveyor landers.
- **MOVES:** VIMO (L, near) holds up three cards like a magician.
- **FEELS:** Cheeky.
- **VIMO:** "Psst! Before the astronauts came, lots of robot scouts helped. Want a quick side trip, or shall we keep going?"
- **TRY IT:** Side-trip cards: **Ranger 8 & 9**, **Lunar Orbiters**, **Surveyor 5, 6 & 7**, and a big **Keep going** button. Each card earns a small stamp; skipping is always fine.

**Side trip — Ranger 8 & 9 (1965)**

- **3D BG:** Black-and-white TV look. An old TV set in a 1960s living room shows the Moon getting closer and closer; then we "enter" the TV and fall with Ranger 9 into Alphonsus crater.
- **MOVES:** VIMO sits on the sofa (S), then jumps into the TV screen.
- **VIMO:** "In 1965, Ranger 8 and Ranger 9 crashed on purpose too. They photographed places where astronauts might land later."
- **MACHINE (Ranger 9, excited old radio voice):** "Millions of people watched on TV as I raced toward the Moon, sending pictures until the very last second!"
- **STATUS CARD:** Crashed on purpose, 1965.
- **SOURCE:** Atlas: Ranger 8 & 9 ([NASA photos](https://images.nasa.gov/search?q=Ranger%209%20Alphonsus&media=image)).

**Side trip — Lunar Orbiters 1–5 (1966–1968)**

- **3D BG:** Five small spacecraft circle the Moon like bees, each painting a strip of map. The map fills in until about 99% is done. Then a famous moment: Earth appears rising over the Moon's edge (real Lunar Orbiter 1 image).
- **MOVES:** VIMO flies along with them (S), holding a paint roller.
- **FEELS:** Amazed.
- **VIMO:** "Five Lunar Orbiters mapped almost the whole Moon, so astronauts could choose safe places to land. One of them took the first picture of Earth seen from near the Moon!"
- **MACHINE (Lunar Orbiter, polite):** "We finished our map, then crashed on purpose so we wouldn't get in the astronauts' way."
- **STATUS CARD:** Crashed on purpose, 1966–68.
- **SOURCE:** Atlas: Lunar Orbiters 1–5 ([NASA photos](https://images.nasa.gov/search?q=Lunar%20Orbiter%201%20Earth&media=image)).

**Side trip — Surveyor 5, 6 & 7 (1967–1968)**

- **3D BG:** Three Surveyor landers in three places: Sea of Tranquility, Sinus Medii, near Tycho crater. Surveyor 6 fires its engines and hops about 2.5 metres sideways.
- **MOVES:** VIMO hops next to Surveyor 6 at the same time (M), then laughs.
- **FEELS:** Funny.
- **VIMO:** "These three tested what Moon soil is made of. And Surveyor 6 did something nobody had done before. Watch!"
- **MACHINE (Surveyor 6, proud):** "I fired my engines, lifted off and landed a few steps away. The first rocket hop ever made on the Moon!"
- **TRY IT:** **Hop like Surveyor 6:** tap to fire, see it hop 2.5 m.
- **STATUS CARD:** Resting on the surface.
- **SOURCE:** Atlas: Surveyor 5, 6 & 7 ([NASA photos](https://images.nasa.gov/search?q=Surveyor%207&media=image)).

### Scene 2.3 — The robot who got a visit (Surveyor 3 + Apollo 12)

This is the emotional heart of the Moon part: proof that an "abandoned" machine was not forgotten.

**Beat 7 — Lonely robot**

### Rules for every 3D model

- Starts rotating slowly by itself (about one turn every 20 seconds) so kids know it's 3D.
- When the reader touches it, auto-rotate stops and they can drag to spin and pinch to zoom.
- A small hand icon wiggles for 2 seconds the first time a model appears; VIMO also says "spin it!".
- Tapping a glowing part opens a short info card (1–2 lines + SOURCE).

## Background prompt library

These prompts make the illustrated or 3D backgrounds, for an image or video generator or as a brief for the 3D developer. Real NASA photos are always used as real photos (never regenerated). Anything generated is labelled "illustration" in the info card, and the AI tool is listed in the submission's Use of AI section.

### Master style (add to the start of every prompt)

> Bright, friendly children's storybook style mixed with soft 3D, like a modern animated film for ages 8–15. Rounded shapes, gentle lighting, clean details, no text, no logos, no real people's faces, no scary or violent elements. Wide 16:9 frame with empty space on one side for a small character and captions. Scientifically accurate shapes for planets and spacecraft.

### Era looks (add one)

- **1960s:** black-and-white with soft TV scan lines and light film grain.
- **Late 1960s–1970s:** faded warm colour film, slightly yellow, soft edges.
- **2000s:** clean digital colours, a bit flat, light camera noise.
- **2020s / today:** crisp, high-detail, rich colours.
